Five machines nobody has built.

Each one is a good idea with a piece missing.

Some are mostly engineering.

The physics is known. The materials are not.

Some need physics we don't have.

This page takes each machine at its word, then marks what it would take.

01Ocean zeppelinsspeculative

Start with the ocean.

Microplastic is in every layer of it, in pieces too small to net and too many to pick up.

Above it, a ring.

A toroidal airship. The donut shape spreads structure and lift evenly around a central hole, which is the claim for its stability.

It hovers low and builds a static charge.

The field does two jobs. First, it holds a liquid film in place across the centre of the ring.

Second, it pulls plastic up out of the water.

Charged microplastic rises off the surface and into the film, which cycles continuously so fresh liquid keeps meeting it.

The film phosphoresces.

It absorbs ordinary sunlight and re-emits it as specific bands of ultraviolet, the wavelengths that break plastic apart.

UV breaks the polymer chains.

Inside the film, long chains split into short ones, then into simple, harmless molecules.

The fish keep swimming.

That is the pitch: clean the sea without touching anything that lives in it.

02Electrogravitic enginespeculative

A chemical rocket throws mass out the back.

Newton's third law, paid for in propellant.

The electrogravitic idea drops the exhaust.

It acts on mass and electromagnetism directly instead.

At the core, a ring of liquid metal.

A toroidal chamber filled with a highly conductive fluid.

Charge it hard. Spin it near the speed of light.

Extreme electrical charge, held in by magnetic containment fields.

Huge angular momentum meets crossing electromagnetic fields.

The claim is that the interaction makes a localised gravitic shear.

A shear that warps spacetime just enough to push one way.

Thrust in one direction. No exhaust.

Nobody has measured this.

The Biefeld–Brown effect, often cited for it, is an ion wind and vanishes in a vacuum. This is the most speculative machine here.

03Personal ion transportreal physics, missing parts

Now shrink a spacecraft engine to fit one person.

Ion propulsion already flies. It just can't lift much.

The catch is thrust.

An ion drive pushes gently, a fraction of a newton. The bar up top shows thrust against weight. A hover needs 1.

Missing piece one: superconductors at room temperature.

Huge currents with no resistance, so no heat. None has been confirmed.

Missing piece two: a carbon nanotube chassis.

Strong for its weight, so the frame barely counts. Nanotube fibres exist. Chassis-sized pieces don't.

Step one: ionise.

Electricity strips an electron from each atom of an onboard noble gas.

Step two: accelerate.

The charged ions feel the electric field between two grids and leave out the back as plasma.

Adjust the variables.

Voltage sets how fast the ions leave. Current sets how many. See if you can find a hover.

Quiet and continuous.

Efficient for the propellant it burns. Not for the power it draws: a hover costs megawatts.

04Whispsspeculative

A hologram is light. A whisp is matter.

One puts an image where there is nothing. The other would put something there.

An ordinary hologram is only light.

Your hand goes straight through it. There is nothing to push back.

Replace the light with billions of nanopixels.

Each is an independent micro-node with its own compute unit and a magnetic interlocking mechanism.

They organise themselves in mid-air.

No mould, no assembler. Each node finds its place and latches onto its neighbours.

Now the shape pushes back.

The electromagnetic bonds resist force. Press a finger in and the bonds along the load hold.

Lean on it.

A shape with mass and structural integrity, made of nothing but a swarm that agrees to hold still.

05Autonomous dronesmostly engineering

Autonomy has two bottlenecks.

A human operator, and a central server that every decision has to travel to and from.

The cost is latency.

Each round trip to the centre takes time. For a fast swarm, that delay is where things go wrong.

So build the computer into the airframe.

Silicon-grade lithography, applied directly to the aerodynamic surfaces. The chassis itself becomes the processor.

Every unit is a supercomputer.

With ballistic guidance on top, it can run split-second trajectory calculations on board.

Now add a thousand more.

Each talks only to the ones beside it. Nothing broadcasts orders.

A threat arrives. Nobody is told.

Each unit evades for itself. The swarm parts around it and closes up behind.

One fluid intelligence, with no one in charge.

Thousands in step, with no external command signal to lose.

06Together

Five machines, five rings and swarms.

Each hangs on one missing piece.

What each one needs.

zeppelinA film that phosphoresces sunlight into plastic-breaking UV, held by charge at sea scale. UV photocatalysis is real, in labs.
graviticA thrust effect that survives in a vacuum. None has been shown.
ion podRoom-temperature superconductors and a megawatt power source. Ion drives themselves fly.
whispsBillions of self-latching micro-nodes. Modular robots exist, at much larger sizes.
dronesCompute built into the airframe. Swarms fly. Chassis-as-computer does not.

Some are a materials problem. One is a physics problem.

Which is which is most of the interesting part.
