Five machines nobody has built.

Each one is a good idea with a piece missing.

Some are mostly engineering.

The physics is known. The materials are not.

Some need physics we don't have.

This page takes each machine at its word, then marks what it would take.

01Ocean zeppelinsspeculative

Start with the ocean.

Nanoplastic is everywhere near the surface, in pieces far too small to net.

Above it, a ring.

A toroidal airship, flying at height. It never needs to go near the water.

At the centre, a bubble.

A thin film of liquid, held in the air by static charge. The ring exists to make that bubble and protect it.

The film phosphoresces.

It absorbs ordinary sunlight and re-emits it as specific bands of ultraviolet.

The light lands as a zone of intense UV.

The airship never touches the plastic. It only lights up the water near the surface.

UV breaks the polymer chains.

Inside the zone, long chains split into short ones, then into simple, harmless molecules.

In enough wind, the ring blows more bubbles.

Each drifting bubble is another film catching sunlight.

02Electrogravitic enginespeculative

A chemical rocket throws mass out the back.

Newton's third law, paid for in propellant.

The electrogravitic idea drops the exhaust.

It acts on mass and electromagnetism directly instead.

At the core, a hollow ring of liquid metal.

A toroidal tube. The liquid inside is an alloy that is mostly element 115, and it flows round in a circle.

Charge it hard. Spin it near the speed of light.

Extreme electrical charge, held in by magnetic containment fields.

Huge angular momentum meets crossing electromagnetic fields.

The claim is that the interaction makes a localised gravitic shear.

A shear that warps spacetime just enough to push one way.

Thrust in one direction. No exhaust.

Nobody has measured this.

Element 115, moscovium, has only been made a few atoms at a time, and decays in under a second. The Biefeld–Brown effect, often cited here, is an ion wind that vanishes in a vacuum.

03Personal ion transportreal physics, missing parts

Now a single-seat pod that flies on air.

No tank of propellant. It pushes on the oxygen and nitrogen around it.

The engine is a tunnel lined with rings.

Thin copper rings discharge static electricity toward aluminium collector rings further down the tunnel.

The current alternates, at high frequency.

Half the cycle drives negative oxygen ions toward the collector. The other half drives positive nitrogen ions the same way.

The tunnel wall pulses magnetic fields.

Strong pulses that boost the electrostatic effect. The Hall effect is part of the idea.

The whole tube is bathed in UV.

Pulsing between wavelengths that react with oxygen and wavelengths that react with nitrogen, so there are more ions to push.

Two breakthroughs make it work for a person.

Carbon-nanotube magnets for strong, light fields. Room-temperature superconductors to carry the current without heat. Neither exists yet.

Adjust the variables.

Plain ion wind first. Then turn up the claimed assist from the magnets and UV, and see what it takes to hover.

Ion wind already flies.

Small ion-wind aircraft manage a few newtons per kilowatt. Hovering a person at that rate takes over half a megawatt.

04Whispsspeculative

A hologram is light. A whisp is matter.

One puts an image where there is nothing. The other would put something there.

An ordinary hologram is only light.

Your hand goes straight through it. There is nothing to push back.

A whisp is a tiny cube of a drone.

A little compute, a little electromagnetics, and the means to coordinate with its neighbours. Made by lithography, the way a silicon chip is.

Early versions skip the compute.

Dumb pixels hang between two smart bay stations, which hold and power them electromagnetically. Together they make a cylindrical screen.

The full version organises itself in mid-air.

No bays. Each whisp finds its place and latches onto its neighbours, electromagnetically.

Now the shape pushes back.

The bonds resist force. Press a finger in and the bonds along the load hold.

Lean on it.

A screen with mass and structural integrity, made of a swarm that agrees to hold still.

05Autonomous droneswhisps, scaled up

Drones are whisps, scaled up.

The same idea at a bigger size: small lithographed units that coordinate with each other. Arguably more useful here, where each unit can carry more.

The old way has two bottlenecks.

A human operator, and a central server that every decision travels to and from. Each round trip costs time, and for a fast swarm that delay is where things go wrong.

So build the computer into the airframe.

Silicon-grade lithography, applied directly to the aerodynamic surfaces, exactly as in a whisp. The chassis itself becomes the processor.

Every unit is a supercomputer.

With ballistic guidance on top, it can run split-second trajectory calculations on board.

Now add a thousand more.

Each talks only to the ones beside it. Nothing broadcasts orders.

A threat arrives. Nobody is told.

Each unit evades for itself. The swarm parts around it and closes up behind.

One fluid intelligence, with no one in charge.

Thousands in step, with no external command signal to lose.

06Together

Five machines, five rings and swarms.

Each hangs on one missing piece.

What each one needs.

zeppelinA film that phosphoresces sunlight into plastic-breaking UV, held as a bubble. UV does degrade plastic; the film is the missing part.
graviticA stable liquid alloy of element 115, and a thrust effect that survives in a vacuum. Moscovium lasts under a second. No such thrust has been shown.
ion podNanotube magnets and room-temperature superconductors. Ion wind flies today at a few newtons per kW.
whispsBillions of lithographed micro-drones that coordinate electromagnetically. Chip-scale robots exist; swarms of them at this size do not.
dronesCompute lithographed into the airframe, as in whisps but larger. Swarms fly; chassis-as-computer does not.

Some are a materials problem. One is a physics problem.

Which is which is most of the interesting part.
